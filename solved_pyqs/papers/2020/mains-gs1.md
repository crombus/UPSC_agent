## Q1. The rock-cut architecture represents one of the most important sources of our knowledge of early Indian art and history. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Indian Art and Culture → [Mauryan, Buddhist, Jain and Rock-Cut Heritage](../../topics/art-and-culture/mauryan-buddhist-jain-and-rock-cut-heritage.md) → Rock-cut architecture as historical evidence · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss evidentiary value  
**Primary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Architecture → Rock-cut heritage  
**Secondary classification:** Ancient History → Material sources and patronage  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rock-cut-architecture-source-early-art-history`  
**Demand:** Explain what rock-cut monuments reveal about art and history, using named sites while qualifying the limits of material evidence.

**Compact framework:** Chronology → artistic technique → religion → economy/patronage → social contacts → source limitation.

### Model answer

Rock-cut monuments are durable archives spanning the Mauryan age to medieval India.

- The Barabar caves display Mauryan polish and patronage by Ashoka and Dasharatha to the Ajivikas.
- Buddhist chaityas and viharas at Bhaja, Karle, Ajanta and Kanheri trace the evolution from timber-inspired forms to elaborate pillars, façades, stupas and monastic complexes.
- Inscriptions identify rulers, merchants, guilds and lay donors, revealing trade routes and the social financing of religion. Karle’s links with western Deccan commerce are illustrative.
- Ajanta’s paintings illuminate dress, court life, music and narrative conventions; Ellora’s Buddhist, Hindu and Jain caves show religious plurality and changing patronage.
- The monolithic Kailasa temple demonstrates advanced planning, excavation and sculptural technology.

However, these elite and sacred sites represent selected regions and patrons; inscriptions, texts and archaeology must supplement them.

**Conclusion:** Rock-cut architecture connects aesthetic change with religion, technology, trade and political authority, making it indispensable—but not self-sufficient—for reconstructing early India.

**Why this earns marks:** It discusses multiple evidentiary dimensions through named monuments and includes source criticism.

## Q2. Pala period is the most significant phase in the history of Buddhism in India. Enumerate.
**Primary route:** Ancient History → [Post-Gupta, Harsha and Eastern India](../../topics/ancient-history/post-gupta-harsha-and-eastern-india.md) → Pala Buddhism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Enumerate significance  
**Primary classification:** Ancient History → Pala period → Buddhism  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Monasteries, manuscripts and trans-Asian exchange  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pala-period-significance-buddhism-india`  
**Demand:** Enumerate the principal institutional, doctrinal, artistic and international contributions of Pala rule, without accepting the superlative uncritically.

**Compact framework:** Patronage → mahaviharas → doctrine → art/texts → trans-Asian transmission → qualification.

### Model answer

The Palas made eastern India the final great institutional centre of Indian Buddhism from the eighth to the twelfth century.

- Gopala, Dharmapala and Devapala patronised Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism while tolerating other faiths.
- Dharmapala founded Vikramashila; Pala support sustained Nalanda, Odantapuri, Somapura and Jagaddala in a *mahavihara* network.
- These institutions developed philosophy, logic, ritual, grammar and medicine and attracted monks from across Asia.
- Teachers such as Atisha Dipankara carried Indian Buddhist learning to Tibet; links extended to Nepal and Southeast Asia.
- Pala bronze sculpture, stone images and illustrated palm-leaf manuscripts established an influential eastern Indian artistic idiom.
- Royal grants and monastic scholarship preserved and systematised Buddhist texts during a period of contraction elsewhere in India.

Yet “most significant” is contestable: the Buddha’s age, Mauryan missions and Kushana-era doctrinal developments were equally foundational.

**Conclusion:** The Pala phase was significant as Buddhism’s last Indian scholastic and trans-Asian bridge, rather than its uniquely decisive phase.

**Why this earns marks:** It enumerates institutions, teachers, doctrines and art, then qualifies the proposition.

## Q3. Evaluate the policies of Lord Curzon and their long term implications on the national movement.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Militant Nationalism and Swadeshi](../../topics/modern-history/militant-nationalism-and-swadeshi.md) → Curzon and nationalist radicalisation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Evaluate policies and consequences  
**Primary classification:** Modern History → Curzon’s viceroyalty → National movement  
**Secondary classification:** Colonial governance → Administrative centralisation and education  
**Proposed topic slug:** `lord-curzon-policies-national-movement-implications`  
**Demand:** Assess Curzon’s principal measures and causally connect them to the long-term transformation of Indian nationalism.

**Compact framework:** Administrative policy → partition → nationalist response → organisational/ideological legacy → balanced verdict.

### Model answer

Curzon combined administrative activism with an openly imperial and centralising outlook.

- The Calcutta Corporation Act reduced elected Indian influence in municipal government.
- The Indian Universities Act, 1904 tightened official control over universities, provoking educated opinion.
- The Official Secrets Act strengthened colonial secrecy, while Curzon’s statements questioning Indian capacity deepened racial resentment.
- His police, irrigation and archaeological measures improved administration and conservation, but remained paternalist.
- Bengal’s partition in 1905 was defended administratively but perceived as an attempt to divide Bengali nationalism.

The anti-partition agitation transformed politics: swadeshi, boycott, national education and passive resistance widened participation and linked economic action with political protest. It strengthened militant nationalism, stimulated indigenous enterprise and spread politics beyond petitioning elites. It also sharpened Congress divisions and contributed to communal mobilisation.

**Conclusion:** Curzon sought to consolidate empire, but especially through Bengal’s partition he unintentionally accelerated mass, assertive and economically grounded nationalism.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates mixed administrative measures while tracing their long-term political consequences.

## Q4. Discuss the geophysical characteristics of Circum-Pacific Zone.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Vulcanism and Earthquakes](../../topics/geography/vulcanism-and-earthquakes.md) → Circum-Pacific belt · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss characteristics  
**Primary classification:** Geography → Plate tectonics → Circum-Pacific Zone  
**Secondary classification:** Disaster geography → Earthquake, volcano and tsunami hazards  
**Proposed topic slug:** `circum-pacific-zone-geophysical-characteristics`  
**Demand:** Describe the zone’s location, tectonic configuration, landforms and hazard profile through plate-tectonic mechanisms.

**Compact framework:** Spatial belt → convergent margins → seismicity → volcanism/landforms → exceptions and hazards.

### Model answer

The Circum-Pacific Zone, or “Ring of Fire”, is a horseshoe-shaped tectonic belt around the Pacific Ocean.

- It follows Pacific and adjoining plate margins from the Andes through North America, the Aleutians, Japan, the Philippines and Indonesia to New Zealand.
- Convergence predominates: dense oceanic lithosphere subducts beneath continental or other oceanic plates, creating deep-sea trenches, Benioff earthquake zones and volcanic arcs.
- Representative features include the Peru–Chile and Mariana trenches, Andes and Cascades continental arcs, and Japanese and Aleutian island arcs.
- The belt experiences shallow-to-deep earthquakes, explosive andesitic volcanism, crustal deformation and mountain building.
- Megathrust rupture and submarine displacement generate destructive tsunamis, as around Japan and Indonesia.
- Transform boundaries, notably California’s San Andreas system, add major seismicity without typical subduction volcanism.

It is therefore not a perfectly continuous volcanic ring; boundary type varies locally.

**Conclusion:** The zone’s exceptional dynamism results from interacting convergent and transform plate margins around the Pacific basin.

**Why this earns marks:** It links mapped features and hazards to specific tectonic processes.

## Q5. The process of desertification does not have climatic boundaries. Justify with examples.
**Primary route:** Environment and Ecology → [Desertification, UNCCD and Land Degradation](../../topics/environment/desertification-unccd-and-land-degradation.md) → Desertification beyond deserts · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Justify with examples  
**Primary classification:** Environment and Ecology → Land degradation → Desertification  
**Secondary classification:** Geography → Human–environment interaction  
**Proposed topic slug:** `desertification-without-climatic-boundaries`  
**Demand:** Show that desertification is land degradation across drylands and can advance through human and climatic drivers outside hot deserts.

**Compact framework:** Definition → multi-climatic drivers → Indian examples → global examples → qualification.

### Model answer

Desertification is persistent land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas; it is not simply the outward spread of hot deserts.

- Overgrazing and fuelwood removal strip vegetation in semi-arid Rajasthan and the Sahel, accelerating wind erosion.
- Faulty irrigation causes waterlogging and salinisation in productive tracts, including Punjab, Haryana and the Indus basin.
- Deforestation, mining and unsuitable cultivation generate sheet and gully erosion in central India and the Chambal ravines.
- Shortened shifting-cultivation fallows can degrade humid and sub-humid slopes in Northeast India.
- Intensive farming, groundwater depletion and drought reduce soil organic matter and productivity across climatic regions.
- Climate change intensifies heat, wildfire and rainfall variability, shifting degradation beyond desert margins.

Not every dry spell constitutes desertification; the decline must be sustained and arise from interacting climatic and human pressures.

**Conclusion:** Because soils and land use—not climate alone—govern degradation, desertification crosses climatic and administrative boundaries.

**Why this earns marks:** It justifies the claim through mechanisms and geographically varied examples.

## Q6. How will the melting of Himalayan glaciers have a far-reaching impact on the water resources of India?
**Primary route:** Geography → [Himalayan Glaciers and GLOF](../../topics/geography/himalayan-glaciers-and-glof.md) → Glacier retreat and water security · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Explain far-reaching impacts  
**Primary classification:** Geography → Cryosphere → Himalayan glaciers  
**Secondary classification:** Water resources → River flows, hazards and livelihoods  
**Proposed topic slug:** `himalayan-glacier-melting-india-water-resources`  
**Demand:** Trace short- and long-term effects on river discharge, seasonality, hazards, groundwater, sectors and interstate or transboundary security.

**Compact framework:** Initial excess flow → long-term decline → seasonal shift → hazards → sectoral and governance effects → qualification.

### Model answer

Himalayan glaciers are long-term stores supplementing snowmelt and monsoon-fed Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems.

- Accelerated melting can initially increase summer discharge, erosion and sediment loads, stressing reservoirs and river infrastructure.
- Expanding moraine-dammed lakes raise the risk of glacial-lake-outburst floods, threatening mountain settlements and hydropower.
- As glacier mass declines, dry-season and drought-year buffering weakens, especially in glacier-dependent upper Indus tributaries.
- Earlier snowmelt changes flow seasonality, creating a mismatch with irrigation, drinking-water and hydropower demand.
- Altered sediment and temperature regimes affect aquatic ecology and reservoir capacity.
- Reduced dependable flows can intensify groundwater extraction in the plains, food insecurity and interstate or transboundary competition.

Impacts will vary: the Ganga and Brahmaputra receive substantial monsoon rainfall, so glacier loss does not imply their immediate disappearance. Rainfall change, snow cover, groundwater and basin management also shape outcomes.

**Conclusion:** Glacier retreat converts a relatively stable frozen reserve into greater near-term hazard and long-term hydrological uncertainty.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes timescales, basins and direct from cascading impacts.

## Q7. Account for the present location of iron and steel industries away from the source of raw material, by giving examples.
**Primary route:** Geography → [Industries and Industrial Regions](../../topics/geography/industries-and-industrial-regions.md) → Changing location of steel industries · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Account for with examples  
**Primary classification:** Geography → Industrial location → Iron and steel  
**Secondary classification:** Economic geography → Ports, markets and technology  
**Proposed topic slug:** `iron-steel-industry-away-raw-material-sources`  
**Demand:** Explain why modern steel plants can be market-, port- or scrap-oriented rather than tied to coal and ore fields.

**Compact framework:** Traditional pull → transport change → material efficiency → market/scrap/port pull → examples → conditionality.

### Model answer

Early integrated steel plants clustered near bulky coal and iron ore because transport costs dominated location. Modern conditions have weakened that constraint.

- Railways and bulk carriers have reduced long-distance freight costs.
- Beneficiation and higher efficiency lower raw-input weight per unit of steel.
- Electric-arc and induction furnaces can use scrap or direct-reduced iron, making plants less dependent on coking-coalfields.
- Coastal plants can import high-grade ore and coking coal and export finished steel efficiently. Japan’s major steel complexes and South Korea’s POSCO at Pohang illustrate port orientation.
- Market proximity reduces finished-product costs and supports automobile, engineering and construction clusters.
- In India, Visakhapatnam reflects coastal advantages, while mini-steel plants occur near markets and scrap supplies around industrial regions.

Nevertheless, large integrated plants such as Jamshedpur and Bhilai retain raw-material advantages.

**Conclusion:** Steel location has shifted from a single raw-material logic toward a transport–port–market–technology calculus.

**Why this earns marks:** It explains the relocation mechanisms and supplies Indian and global examples.

## Q8. Has caste lost its relevance in understanding the multi-cultural Indian Society? Elaborate your answer with illustrations.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Caste System: Structure and Contemporary Dynamics](../../topics/indian-society/caste-system-structure-and-contemporary-dynamics.md) → Contemporary relevance of caste · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Elaborate with illustrations  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Caste → Continuity and change  
**Secondary classification:** Social change → Class, politics and multiculturalism  
**Proposed topic slug:** `caste-relevance-multicultural-indian-society`  
**Demand:** Judge whether caste remains analytically relevant, illustrating both persistence and transformation without reducing Indian diversity to caste alone.

**Compact framework:** Declining ritual rigidity → persistent marriage/inequality → political and economic adaptation → intersections → balanced verdict.

### Model answer

Caste has changed but remains indispensable—though insufficient—for understanding multicultural India.

- Urbanisation, education and mobility have weakened hereditary occupation, commensality restrictions and ritual hierarchy.
- Yet matrimonial choices remain strongly caste-endogamous, and caste networks influence housing, employment referrals and social capital.
- Unequal ownership of land, sanitation work and exposure to violence show the material persistence of hierarchy; constitutional safeguards and reservations respond to these inherited disadvantages.
- Democratic politics has converted caste into an instrument of representation and bargaining. Dalit assertion, backward-class mobilisation and caste associations demonstrate agency rather than mere ritual status.
- Migration and markets can blur caste publicly while reproducing networks informally.
- Caste also intersects with class, gender, tribe, religion, language and region: a Dalit woman’s experience cannot be explained by caste alone.

**Conclusion:** Caste has lost some ritual rigidity, not social relevance. It must be studied as a changing institution within India’s wider plural and intersectional order.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “has it lost relevance?” through balanced continuity-and-change illustrations.

## Q9. COVID-19 pandemic accelerated class inequalities and poverty in India. Comment.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Poverty and Developmental Issues](../../topics/indian-society/poverty-and-developmental-issues.md) → Pandemic inequality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q9 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment critically  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Poverty and inequality → COVID-19  
**Secondary classification:** Economy → Informality, digital divide and social protection  
**Proposed topic slug:** `covid19-class-inequality-poverty-india`  
**Demand:** Explain the unequal transmission of the pandemic shock, recognise mitigating measures and deliver a qualified judgment.

**Compact framework:** Unequal exposure → income shock → capability divide → vulnerable groups → relief → structural verdict.

### Model answer

COVID-19 was a common health shock but an unequal social and economic experience.

- Lockdowns abruptly removed earnings of casual workers, street vendors, domestic workers and small enterprises, while many salaried professionals could work remotely.
- Migrant workers faced job loss, insecure housing and costly journeys because welfare and rations were weakly portable.
- Poor households had fewer savings; medical expenditure and indebtedness deepened vulnerability.
- School closures exposed the device, connectivity and learning-support divide.
- Women experienced job losses, unpaid care and domestic violence; caste and occupation further shaped exposure.
- Asset-owning and digitally connected groups recovered faster, whereas informal workers often sold assets or reduced nutrition, risking longer-term poverty.

Free foodgrains, cash support, MGNREGA and later portability measures cushioned hardship, though coverage and adequacy varied.

**Conclusion:** The pandemic did not create all inequalities; it magnified pre-existing informality, weak social security and unequal capabilities, producing a sharply K-shaped burden and recovery.

**Why this earns marks:** It links class inequality to mechanisms, vulnerable groups and policy qualification without unsupported statistics.

## Q10. Do you agree that regionalism in India appears to be a consequence of rising cultural assertiveness? Argue.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Unity in Diversity, Communalism and Regionalism](../../topics/indian-society/unity-diversity-communalism-regionalism.md) → Cultural assertiveness and regionalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q1–Q10 · Official Q10 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Argue a qualified position  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Regionalism → Cultural identity  
**Secondary classification:** Federalism → Developmental imbalance and political mobilisation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `regionalism-cultural-assertiveness-india`  
**Demand:** Evaluate cultural assertiveness as one cause of regionalism against economic, political and administrative explanations.

**Compact framework:** Cultural basis → examples → material and political drivers → constructive/destructive forms → verdict.

### Model answer

Cultural assertiveness is a major expression of Indian regionalism, but not its sole cause.

- Linguistic pride drove the Andhra movement and state reorganisation in 1956, strengthening the Union through accommodation.
- Tamil mobilisation resisted perceived Hindi imposition; movements in Assam and the Northeast have invoked language, ethnicity, land and indigenous culture.
- Recognition of local histories, scripts and customary institutions can protect diversity and correct domination.

However, identity becomes politically powerful when joined to material grievances:

- uneven development, employment competition and resource-sharing disputes;
- perceived neglect by distant capitals;
- migration anxieties and “sons of the soil” politics;
- elite mobilisation through regional parties.

Thus Telangana combined a regional identity with claims of unequal irrigation, jobs and expenditure. Regionalism can deepen federal representation, but exclusionary forms may stigmatise migrants or encourage secessionism.

**Conclusion:** Cultural assertiveness supplies regionalism’s symbols and emotional vocabulary; political economy and federal governance usually determine its intensity and direction.

**Why this earns marks:** It argues rather than merely agrees, balancing identity with material causation.

## Q11. Indian philosophy and tradition played a significant role in conceiving and shaping the monuments and their art in India. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Indian Art and Culture → [Religion, Philosophy and Cultural Synthesis](../../topics/art-and-culture/religion-philosophy-and-cultural-synthesis.md) → Philosophical foundations of monuments · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss philosophical influence  
**Primary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Monuments and art → Philosophical symbolism  
**Secondary classification:** Religion → Buddhist, Jain and Hindu traditions  
**Proposed topic slug:** `indian-philosophy-tradition-monuments-art`  
**Demand:** Explain how philosophical and ritual concepts shaped spatial plans, symbols, iconography and artistic programmes, with named examples and material qualifications.

**Compact framework:** Sacred space → Buddhist/Jain forms → Hindu cosmology → iconography/performance → synthesis → patronage and technology qualification.

### Model answer

Indian monuments are not merely engineered structures; their plans and images often translate philosophical and ritual ideas into space.

**Buddhist traditions**

- The stupa embodies the Buddha’s presence through relics and symbols. Its dome, axis and circumambulatory path organise memory, cosmic order and *pradakshina*, as at Sanchi.
- Chaityas such as Karle create a processional route toward the stupa, while viharas materialise the monastic *sangha*.
- Ajanta’s Jataka paintings communicate compassion, merit and the bodhisattva ideal through narrative art.

**Jain traditions**

- Images of Tirthankaras express detachment, meditation and spiritual victory. The ordered geometry and refined marble of Dilwara temples support contemplative sacred space.

**Hindu traditions**

- The *garbhagriha* represents a concentrated divine centre; the vertical *shikhara* or *vimana* evokes the cosmic mountain. Temple movement from outer *mandapas* to the sanctum symbolises passage from the manifest world toward ultimate reality.
- Vastupurusha-mandala principles organise orientation and proportion. Khajuraho and Chola temples integrate deities, guardians, dancers and worldly life into a larger cosmic whole.
- Nataraja’s dance presents creation, preservation, destruction, concealment and grace in one icon.

Regional traditions also absorbed local cults, Islamic forms and royal meanings. Ellora’s multi-religious caves show coexistence and shared craftsmanship.

Yet philosophy alone did not determine monuments: political legitimation, guild skill, materials, economy and patronage shaped what was built.

**Conclusion:** Indian philosophy supplied monuments with symbolic grammar and ritual purpose, while society and power translated that grammar into diverse regional art.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects specific concepts to architectural and artistic features, then qualifies idealist causation.

## Q12. Persian literary sources of medieval India reflect the spirit of the age. Comment.
**Primary route:** Medieval History → [Mughal Society, Economy and Culture](../../topics/medieval-history/mughal-society-economy-and-culture.md) → Persian sources and historical method · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Comment with source criticism  
**Primary classification:** Medieval History → Persian literature → Sources  
**Secondary classification:** Indian Art and Culture → Court culture and intellectual exchange  
**Proposed topic slug:** `persian-literary-sources-medieval-india-spirit-age`  
**Demand:** Show how varied Persian texts reveal political ideals, administration, society and culture, while evaluating their courtly biases and silences.

**Compact framework:** Source types → political-administrative world → society/economy → cultural synthesis → limitations → verdict.

### Model answer

Persian became a major language of court, administration and high culture under the Delhi Sultanates and Mughals. Its literature therefore captures important aspirations and contradictions of medieval India.

**Political and administrative spirit**

- Minhaj-us-Siraj’s *Tabaqat-i-Nasiri* and Barani’s *Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi* record dynastic politics, noble factions and ideals of kingship. Barani’s normative judgments also reveal elite anxieties about hierarchy.
- Abul Fazl’s *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari* present Akbar’s sovereignty, *sulh-i-kul*, mansabdari, revenue arrangements, provinces, occupations and court culture.
- Badauni’s *Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh* supplies a critical orthodox response, exposing ideological contest within the Mughal elite.

**Social, economic and cultural life**

- Babur’s *Baburnama*, translated into Persian, observes landscapes, crops, towns and customs.
- Amir Khusrau’s writings reflect Indo-Persian literary creativity, urban life and interaction with Indian languages and music.
- Court histories, biographies, Sufi *malfuzat* and letters illuminate trade, learning, religious debate, etiquette and patronage.
- Persian translations of the *Mahabharata*, Upanishads and other Sanskrit works under Akbar and Dara Shikoh reveal intellectual encounter and cultural synthesis.

However, most authors were male, literate and court-linked. They often privileged rulers, wars and urban elites, underrepresenting peasants, women, vernacular worlds and dissent. Panegyric, genre conventions and moral purposes require comparison with inscriptions, regional literature, archaeology and travellers.

**Conclusion:** Persian sources reflect the age’s imperial institutions, social hierarchies and composite cultural exchanges, but through selective elite lenses rather than as transparent mirrors.

**Why this earns marks:** It uses named texts for multiple dimensions and makes source criticism central to the comment.

## Q13. Since the decade of the 1920s, the national movement acquired various ideological strands and thereby expanded its social base. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Modern History → [Swarajists and Revolutionaries in the 1920s](../../topics/modern-history/swarajists-and-revolutionaries-in-the-1920s.md) → Ideological diversification · **Secondary owner:** [Left, Peasant, Workers’ and States’ Peoples’ Movements](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Modern-Indian-History/advanced/23_Left-Peasant-Workers-and-States-Peoples-Movements.md) · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss strands and social expansion  
**Primary classification:** Modern History → National movement → Ideological diversification  
**Secondary classification:** Social history → Peasants, workers, women, youth and princely states  
**Proposed topic slug:** `national-movement-1920s-ideological-strands-social-base`  
**Demand:** Identify major ideological strands emerging or consolidating from the 1920s and explain how each widened participation, while noting tensions and exclusions.

**Compact framework:** Gandhian mass politics → constitutionalists → revolutionaries → socialist/communist mobilisation → social and regional currents → limits.

### Model answer

From the 1920s, anti-colonial nationalism ceased to be primarily an educated-middle-class constitutional movement and became a broad, contested coalition.

**Gandhian current:** Non-Cooperation joined *satyagraha*, swadeshi and constructive work with the Khilafat issue. It drew peasants, traders, students, women and sections of ulema into national politics. Civil Disobedience later mobilised salt-makers, forest communities and urban groups.

**Constitutional nationalism:** After Non-Cooperation’s suspension, C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru’s Swarajists entered legislatures to obstruct colonial governance, retaining those who preferred institutional politics.

**Revolutionary republicanism:** The Hindustan Republican Association and later HSRA moved from individual action toward republican and socialist language. Bhagat Singh’s ideas attracted radical youth and reframed freedom as social transformation.

**Left and class politics:** Communist groups, the Congress Socialist Party, trade unions and organisations such as the All India Kisan Sabha connected independence with workers’ rights, agrarian reform and anti-imperialism.

**Other widening currents:** Ambedkarite mobilisation placed caste oppression and political representation on the agenda. Women participated in picketing, salt law violation and underground activity. Praja Mandals carried nationalism into princely states; tribal and peasant movements often combined local grievances with Congress politics.

This diversity generated tensions—class versus national unity, communal mobilisation, caste representation, methods and constitutional strategy. Congress leadership did not always fully absorb autonomous subaltern demands.

**Conclusion:** Ideological plurality expanded nationalism’s geography and social constituency, transforming freedom into competing visions of political, social and economic emancipation.

**Why this earns marks:** It links each named ideological strand to the social groups and issues it mobilised.

## Q14. The interlinking of rivers can provide viable solutions to the multi-dimensional inter-related problems of droughts, floods and interrupted navigation. Critically examine.
**Primary route:** Geography → [India: Drainage and River Interlinking](../../topics/geography/india-drainage-and-river-interlinking.md) → River interlinking · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate–Difficult · Demand tag: Critically examine viability  
**Primary classification:** Geography → Water resources → River interlinking  
**Secondary classification:** Environment and federalism → Basin ecology and water governance  
**Proposed topic slug:** `river-interlinking-drought-flood-navigation-critical-examination`  
**Demand:** Test the claimed drought, flood and navigation benefits against hydrological, ecological, social, economic and federal constraints before giving a conditional verdict.

**Compact framework:** Claimed multi-purpose benefits → hydrological limits → ecological/social costs → governance/economics → alternatives and conditions.

### Model answer

River interlinking seeks to transfer water from identified “surplus” basins to “deficit” basins through reservoirs and canals. Its appeal lies in integrating several water problems.

**Potential benefits**

- Stored monsoon flows could supplement irrigation and drinking water in drought-prone areas and moderate some flood peaks.
- More dependable flows may support inland navigation, hydropower and regional economic integration.
- Projects such as Ken–Betwa seek to address spatial and seasonal water imbalance.

**Why viability is contested**

- “Surplus” and “deficit” are not permanent categories. Climate change, ecological flow needs, groundwater interaction and future basin demand alter water availability.
- Floods often arise from intense local rainfall, encroached floodplains and drainage failure; distant transfers cannot eliminate these causes. Large reservoirs may absorb selected peaks but not all basin floods.
- Dams and canals fragment rivers, alter sediment transport, affect deltas and fisheries, and may submerge forests and wildlife habitat.
- Displacement and livelihood loss impose unequal costs on tribal and rural communities.
- High capital, pumping and maintenance requirements create opportunity costs. Navigation additionally requires terminals, channel depth and viable freight, not water transfer alone.
- Interstate and international rivers raise federal, legal and diplomatic disputes.

A basin-specific approach is preferable: watershed restoration, aquifer recharge, crop diversification, tank revival, efficient irrigation, wetland and floodplain protection, and demand management. Links should proceed only after transparent cumulative assessment, environmental-flow guarantees, consent-based rehabilitation and climate-tested hydrology.

**Conclusion:** Interlinking can selectively complement water management, but cannot serve as a universal engineering solution to drought, flood and navigation.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates every claimed benefit against mechanism-specific limitations and proposes a qualified alternative framework.

## Q15. Account for the huge flooding of million cities in India including the smart ones like Hyderabad and Pune. Suggest lasting remedial measures.
**Primary route:** Disaster Management → [Riverine Floods and Urban Flood Resilience](../../topics/disaster-management/riverine-floods-and-urban-flood-resilience.md) → Urban flooding · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q15 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Account for and suggest remedies  
**Primary classification:** Disaster Management → Urban floods → Resilience  
**Secondary classification:** Urbanisation → Land use, drainage and governance  
**Proposed topic slug:** `million-cities-urban-flooding-lasting-remedies`  
**Demand:** Explain hazard, exposure and governance causes of severe urban floods and propose structural, ecological and institutional measures that endure.

**Compact framework:** Extreme rainfall → lost drainage/storage → infrastructure and governance failures → city examples → blue-green and risk-governance remedies.

### Model answer

Urban floods result when intense rainfall exceeds a city’s reduced capacity to absorb, store and safely drain water. “Smart” infrastructure does not compensate for unsound land use.

**Causes**

- Climate variability is increasing short-duration, high-intensity rainfall. Hyderabad’s 2020 event and recurrent Pune floods illustrate concentrated downpours.
- Lakes, wetlands and floodplains have been filled or encroached. Construction across Hyderabad’s interconnected tanks and Pune’s Mula–Mutha floodplain removes natural storage.
- Impervious roads and buildings sharply increase runoff and suppress groundwater recharge.
- Drains are undersized, poorly mapped or blocked by solid waste, sewage and construction debris.
- Roads, metro works and layouts interrupt natural channels; uncoordinated reservoir releases can worsen downstream peaks.
- Multiple agencies divide responsibility for planning, drainage, water bodies and disaster response, while outdated rainfall design standards and weak enforcement increase exposure.

**Lasting measures**

1. Map watersheds, drains, wetlands and floodplains at fine scale; prohibit and reverse high-risk encroachment.
2. Restore urban lakes as connected systems, protect buffers and adopt “sponge city” features—permeable surfaces, bioswales, rain gardens and detention basins.
3. Upgrade storm drains using future rainfall projections; separate storm water from sewage and maintain drains before every monsoon.
4. Use real-time rainfall, reservoir and inundation forecasting with ward-level warnings and evacuation plans.
5. Integrate basin-level planning across municipal boundaries, assign one accountable coordinating authority and audit land-use compliance.
6. Apply risk-sensitive building codes, insurance signals and participatory planning, especially for informal settlements.

**Conclusion:** Lasting resilience requires treating drainage, wetlands and floodplains as essential urban infrastructure rather than residual land for construction.

**Why this earns marks:** It separates physical and governance causes and converts them into matched, durable remedies.

## Q16. India has immense potential of solar energy though there are regional variations in its development. Elaborate.
**Primary route:** Environment and Ecology → [Renewable Energy and Green Hydrogen](../../topics/environment/renewable-energy-and-green-hydrogen.md) → Solar potential and regional variation · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Elaborate potential and variation  
**Primary classification:** Environment and Ecology → Renewable energy → Solar power  
**Secondary classification:** Economic geography → Resource, infrastructure and policy variation  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-solar-energy-potential-regional-variation`  
**Demand:** Establish India’s solar potential, explain spatial variation in both resource and realised development, and indicate constraints and region-sensitive policy.

**Compact framework:** National advantages → high/medium resource geography → development clusters → explanatory factors → constraints and differentiated strategy.

### Model answer

India’s tropical location, extensive drylands, large rooftops and dispersed energy demand provide a strong basis for solar power.

**Potential**

- High insolation and many clear-sky days make western and peninsular India particularly suitable for utility-scale photovoltaic and solar-thermal generation.
- Solar can reduce fossil-fuel dependence, serve remote settlements, power irrigation and complement daytime commercial demand.
- Rooftop systems, solar parks, pumps and decentralised mini-grids permit deployment across scales.

**Regional variation**

- Rajasthan and Gujarat combine strong irradiation, relatively available land, grid connectivity and proactive solar-park policies. Bhadla and Charanka exemplify this model.
- Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra have also expanded through policy support, transmission access and suitable sites.
- The Indo-Gangetic plains possess substantial potential but face dense settlement, costly land, fog, dust and seasonal atmospheric pollution.
- Northeast India, the Himalayan states and the west coast experience greater cloudiness, complex terrain or limited contiguous land, favouring rooftops and decentralised systems over very large parks.
- Development also varies with state utility finances, payment security, open-access rules, transmission capacity, land acquisition and administrative competence—not sunlight alone.

**Constraints and way forward**

Intermittency requires storage, flexible grids, forecasting and stronger interstate transmission. Large parks may compete with grazing, farming and fragile ecosystems; panel manufacturing, recycling and water-efficient cleaning also matter. Policy should therefore combine desert solar parks with urban rooftops, agrivoltaics, canal-top systems and locally appropriate mini-grids.

**Conclusion:** India’s solar potential is nationwide, but its optimal form is regional: resource quality must be matched with land, grids, institutions and ecological safeguards.

**Why this earns marks:** It distinguishes physical potential from realised development and explains regional differences with named examples.

## Q17. Examine the status of forest resources of India and its resultant impact on climate change.
**Primary route:** Environment and Ecology → [Forest Types and the Forest Rights Act](../../topics/environment/forest-types-and-forest-rights-act.md) → Forest resources and climate change · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Examine status and climate impact  
**Primary classification:** Environment and Ecology → Forest resources → Climate regulation  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Forest quality, rights and restoration  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-forest-resources-status-climate-change-impact`  
**Demand:** Assess extent, quality and pressures on Indian forests, then explain their two-way relationship with climate change without relying on a single headline statistic.

**Compact framework:** Diverse resource base → area versus quality → pressures → carbon/water/climate services → climate damage feedback → governance priorities.

### Model answer

India’s forests range from tropical evergreen and deciduous formations to thorn, montane and mangrove ecosystems. Their climate significance depends not merely on mapped “forest cover”, but on ecological quality, density and continuity.

**Status**

- India retains extensive forest and tree resources; plantations can increase recorded cover.
- However, dense natural forests face fragmentation from roads, mining, dams, settlement and repeated fire. Open forests and plantations lack the biodiversity and carbon value of mature native ecosystems.
- Northeastern shifting land-use pressures, Himalayan fires, degradation in central Indian tribal landscapes and mangrove stress illustrate region-specific concerns.
- Community dependence on forests for food, fuel, fodder and minor forest produce makes rights and livelihoods integral to conservation.

**Impact on climate change**

- Forests sequester and store carbon; degradation and fire release carbon and weaken India’s mitigation capacity.
- Canopies regulate evapotranspiration, local temperature, rainfall recycling and soil moisture. Watershed forests moderate erosion, runoff and drought vulnerability.
- Mangroves store substantial “blue carbon” and buffer coasts against cyclones and storm surges.
- Biodiverse forests are generally more resilient and sustain adaptation services better than monoculture plantations.

The relationship is reciprocal: warming, altered monsoons, pests, drought and extreme fires can shift species ranges and turn stressed forests from sinks into sources.

Policy should prioritise protection of natural forests, ecological restoration with native species, fire management, wildlife corridors, community tenure and transparent monitoring rather than plantation counts alone.

**Conclusion:** India’s forest resource remains a major climate asset, but degradation and fragmentation can erode both its carbon sink and its adaptation functions.

**Why this earns marks:** It examines quality as well as extent and explains mitigation, adaptation and feedback mechanisms.

## Q18. Is diversity and pluralism in India under threat due to globalisation? Justify your answer.
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Effects of Globalisation on Indian Society](../../topics/indian-society/effects-of-globalisation-on-indian-society.md) → Diversity and pluralism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q18 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Justify a balanced judgment  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Globalisation → Diversity and pluralism  
**Secondary classification:** Culture → Homogenisation, hybridity and identity assertion  
**Proposed topic slug:** `globalisation-threat-diversity-pluralism-india`  
**Demand:** Distinguish diversity from pluralism and assess both homogenising and diversity-enabling effects of globalisation through Indian illustrations.

**Compact framework:** Define terms → threats → enabling/hybrid effects → unequal power → safeguards → qualified verdict.

### Model answer

Diversity denotes the coexistence of different languages, faiths, customs and ways of life; pluralism adds equal respect and institutional accommodation. Globalisation places both under pressure, but its effects are not uniformly destructive.

**Threats**

- Global brands, mass entertainment and platform algorithms can standardise consumption, aspirations and language, marginalising local crafts, cuisines and oral traditions.
- English and dominant Indian languages gain economic value, while smaller languages struggle to reproduce across generations.
- Market-led tourism may commodify rituals and detach cultural forms from communities.
- Concentrated media and digital platforms can amplify stereotypes, polarisation and majoritarian narratives, weakening plural dialogue.
- Unequal competition exposes artisans, small producers and indigenous knowledge to appropriation without fair returns.

**Countervailing effects**

- Digital tools allow regional cinema, music, literature and crafts to reach diasporic and global audiences.
- Global rights discourses have strengthened movements around gender, disability, caste and indigenous identity.
- Migration produces hybrid food, language, fashion and art rather than simple Westernisation.
- Cultural assertiveness can revive local identities; geographical indications and creative industries may support community livelihoods.

The decisive issue is power: communities with connectivity, capital and legal protection can adapt, while vulnerable groups face erasure or tokenisation. Education in mother tongues, cultural archives, plural media, community intellectual rights and non-discriminatory citizenship are therefore essential.

**Conclusion:** Globalisation threatens diversity when market concentration creates homogenisation and exclusion; under democratic and equitable institutions, it can also widen recognition and generate new plural forms.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines both concepts, presents competing causal pathways and justifies a conditional answer.

## Q19. Customs and traditions suppress reason leading to obscurantism. Do you agree?
**Primary route:** Indian Society → [Social Change and Modernisation](../../topics/indian-society/social-change-and-modernisation.md) → Tradition, reason and reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-I · Section Q11–Q20 · Official Q19 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Evaluate normative proposition  
**Primary classification:** Indian Society → Customs and traditions → Reason and obscurantism  
**Secondary classification:** Social reform → Constitutional morality and cultural continuity  
**Proposed topic slug:** `customs-traditions-reason-obscurantism`  
**Demand:** Take a reasoned position on whether tradition inherently suppresses reason, using examples of oppressive customs, reform from within tradition and criteria for legitimate continuity.

**Compact framework:** Clarify concepts → evidence of suppression → rational/adaptive traditions → reform traditions → constitutional test → verdict.

### Model answer

Customs are inherited social practices; obscurantism arises when authority shields beliefs from evidence, criticism and equal human dignity. Some traditions do suppress reason, but tradition as such is not irrational.

**Where the proposition holds**

- Practices justified solely by inherited hierarchy—untouchability, exclusion from temples or wells, caste endogamy enforced through violence, and discriminatory taboos—deny individual agency.
- Patriarchal customs such as child marriage, dowry and restrictions on women’s education have often survived through appeals to honour and sacred authority.
- Witch-branding, miracle cures and resistance to vaccination can turn untested belief into bodily harm.
- When questioning is punished, custom becomes an instrument of power rather than collective wisdom.

**Why blanket rejection is mistaken**

- Traditions also preserve tested ecological and social knowledge: sacred groves, community water harvesting, seasonal practices and diverse craft systems can embody practical reason.
- Indian intellectual traditions contain debate and self-criticism—Buddhist inquiry, Bhakti critiques of ritual hierarchy, and reformers such as Rammohan Roy, Jyotiba Phule, Savitribai Phule and Ambedkar challenged oppression through reasoned argument.
- Customs are dynamic: societies reinterpret marriage, inheritance, worship and community practice in response to law, education and internal reform.

The proper test is not “old versus new” but constitutional morality, evidence, consent, equality and absence of harm. Scientific temper under Article 51A(h) should coexist with cultural freedom, subject to fundamental rights.

**Conclusion:** Customs become obscurantist when insulated from scrutiny and used to perpetuate domination; reflective, revisable and rights-compatible traditions can instead enrich social life.

**Why this earns marks:** It avoids both romanticising and dismissing tradition and supplies a principled constitutional criterion.

**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Education → Digital initiatives  
**Secondary classification:** Governance → Access, quality, administration and inclusion  
**Proposed topic slug:** `digital-initiatives-functioning-education-system-india`  
**Demand:** Explain how named digital initiatives improve teaching, learning, assessment and administration, while evaluating access, pedagogy and institutional constraints.

**Compact framework:** Content platforms → broadcast and repositories → teacher/institution support → administration → pandemic continuity → digital divide and blended way forward.

### Model answer

Digital initiatives have expanded the education system beyond the physical classroom and improved the circulation of content, training and administrative information.

**Contributions**

- **DIKSHA** provides curriculum-linked digital resources and teacher-training content, including QR-linked material in school textbooks.
- **SWAYAM** offers online courses across school, higher and continuing education, while **SWAYAM PRABHA** uses direct-to-home television channels where internet access is weak.
- **e-Pathshala**, the National Digital Library and virtual laboratories widen access to textbooks, reference material and simulations.
- **NISHTHA** supports large-scale teacher professional development.
- Digital classrooms, learning-management systems and video platforms enabled partial educational continuity during COVID-19 and support self-paced revision.
- Online admission, scholarship, attendance, examination and institutional data systems can reduce transaction costs, improve monitoring and make resources more portable.
- Digital repositories permit multilingual reuse and can connect remote learners with specialist teaching.

**Limitations**

Access is unequal by income, gender, disability, region and language. Many households lack devices, stable electricity, connectivity or quiet learning space. Screen-based delivery cannot fully replace teacher interaction, laboratories, peer socialisation or care functions of schools. Weak digital pedagogy may reproduce lectures rather than improve learning; privacy, commercialisation, misinformation and excessive surveillance also require safeguards.

The answer is therefore blended education: invest in public digital infrastructure, shared devices, accessible multilingual content, teacher support, offline and broadcast modes, data protection and regular learning assessment.

**Conclusion:** Digital initiatives improve reach, resilience and administration, but contribute to educational justice only when they supplement capable institutions and close—not widen—the digital divide.

**Why this earns marks:** It names initiatives, links each to a system function and balances contribution with implementation limits.
