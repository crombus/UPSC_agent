## Q1. “There is a need for simplification of procedure for disqualification of persons found guilty of corrupt practices under the Representation of Peoples Act.” Comment.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Election Commission](../../topics/polity/election-commission.md) → Electoral disqualification · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q1 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Comment  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Elections → Corrupt practices and disqualification  
**Canonical owner:** [Election Commission](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/27_Election-Commission.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rpa-corrupt-practices-disqualification`

**Demand decoding:** Distinguish corrupt practices from criminal conviction, identify the delay-producing statutory chain, and support simplification without sacrificing due process.

**Compact framework:** Statutory route → defects → simplification → safeguards.

### Model answer

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 defines bribery, undue influence and specified electoral appeals as “corrupt practices” under Section **123**. When an election court names a person under Section **99**, disqualification under Section **8A** is decided by the President on the Election Commission’s opinion, for up to six years.

The process requires completion of an election petition, specific naming and notice, followed by another decision stage. Litigation may outlast the elected term, weakening deterrence. Section **8**, concerning conviction for listed offences, is distinct.

Reform should provide time-bound election trials, fixed periods for the Commission’s opinion and presidential order, electronic records and reasoned decisions. Special election benches and continuation despite expiry of the House would help. *Abhiram Singh v. C.D. Commachen* (2017) confirms the importance of enforcing Section 123.

**Conclusion:** Simplification should remove duplication and delay, not notice, hearing, judicial review or the presumption of innocence.

**Why this earns marks:** It identifies the exact statutory chain, explains why it fails, and proposes due-process-compatible reform.

## Q2. “Recent amendments to the Right to Information Act will have profound impact on the autonomy and independence of the Information Commission.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Transparency, Accountability, RTI, Grievance Redress and Social Audit](../../topics/governance/transparency-accountability-grievance-redress-and-social-audit.md) → Information Commissions · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q2 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Transparency → RTI and Information Commissions  
**Canonical owners:** [CIC and SIC](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/CIC-and-SIC.md) · [Transparency and Accountability](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/08_Transparency-Accountability-Grievance-Redress-and-Social-Audit.md) · [Statutory Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rti-amendment-information-commission-independence`

**Demand decoding:** Explain the 2019 changes, connect service security with decisional independence, and assess both the government’s justification and institutional risk.

**Compact framework:** Pre-amendment guarantee → amendment → autonomy concern → balanced safeguards.

### Model answer

The RTI Act originally fixed five-year tenure and linked Information Commissioners’ service conditions to constitutional election authorities. The **RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019** made tenure, salaries and service conditions subject to Union rules; the 2019 Rules prescribed three years.

The Government argued that statutory Information Commissions differ from the Election Commission, making equivalence unnecessary. Yet independence also requires insulation from the executive whose records commissioners order disclosed. Union control over conditions of Central and State Commissioners creates perceived dependence, permits future variation by rules and weakens federal autonomy.

Capture is not automatic: statutory adjudicatory powers remain and orders face judicial review. Independence would nevertheless be better secured through fixed tenure, non-diminution of conditions, transparent selection, timely appointments—as stressed in *Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India*—and financial autonomy.

**Conclusion:** Administrative flexibility cannot justify avoidable executive leverage over transparency adjudicators.

**Why this earns marks:** It states the precise amendment, considers both sides and links institutional design to functional independence.

## Q3. How far do you think cooperation, competition and confrontation have shaped the nature of federation in India? Cite some recent examples to validate your answer.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Federal System](../../topics/polity/federal-system.md) → Cooperative, competitive and confrontational federalism · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q3 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: How far; Validate with examples  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Intergovernmental dynamics  
**Canonical owner:** [Federal System](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/12_Federal-System.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `cooperative-competitive-confrontational-federalism`

**Demand decoding:** Give a graded assessment of all three modes, use contemporaneous examples, and explain their combined effect on India’s quasi-federal design.

**Compact framework:** Cooperation → competition → confrontation → qualified assessment.

### Model answer

India’s federation is Union-tilted but politically negotiated; cooperation, competition and confrontation operate simultaneously.

- **Cooperation:** The GST Council under Article **279A**, centrally sponsored schemes and disaster response require joint decisions. COVID-19 demonstrated this interdependence.
- **Competition:** NITI Aayog indices and competition for investment encourage better regulation and services, though unequal capacity creates an uneven field.
- **Confrontation:** Disputes over GST compensation, farm laws, Governors, central agencies and withdrawal of general CBI consent reveal mistrust about encroachment.

The Supreme Court in *S.R. Bommai* restrained arbitrary central domination, while the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions favoured consultation and restraint.

**Conclusion:** Cooperation is indispensable, competition can improve outcomes, and confrontation supplies a check. When it displaces consultation, federalism becomes coercive; stronger Inter-State Council processes and predictable transfers are essential.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers “how far” through three distinct mechanisms, recent examples and a calibrated federal verdict.

## Q4. The judicial systems in India and UK seem to be converging as well as diverging in recent times. Highlight the key points of convergence and divergence between the two nations in terms of their judicial practices.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../topics/polity/comparative-constitutional-schemes.md) → India–UK judiciary · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q4 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Highlight comparison  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Comparative Constitution → Judicial systems  
**Canonical owners:** [Comparative Constitutional Schemes](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Comparative-Constitutional-Schemes.md) · [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/18_Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `india-uk-judicial-convergence-divergence`

**Demand decoding:** Compare recent institutional and doctrinal practices, not merely constitutional forms; preserve the decisive parliamentary-sovereignty distinction.

**Compact framework:** Common heritage → convergence → divergence → synthesis.

### Model answer

Both systems share common-law reasoning, precedent, adversarial procedure and judicial independence. The UK Supreme Court, operational since 2009 under the Constitutional Reform Act, 2005, separated the highest court from Parliament; both judiciaries increasingly use proportionality and review executive action.

Important divergences remain:

- India has a written supreme Constitution, enforceable fundamental rights and strong judicial review; its courts may invalidate legislation and apply the basic-structure doctrine.
- UK parliamentary sovereignty prevents courts from striking down primary legislation; under the Human Rights Act, 1998, they may issue a declaration of incompatibility.
- India’s higher judges are appointed through the collegium evolved in the Judges Cases; the UK uses a statutory Judicial Appointments Commission-led process.
- Indian public-interest litigation and Article **32** jurisdiction provide wider direct access than conventional UK standing rules, despite their liberalisation.

**Conclusion:** Practices converge around institutional independence and rights review, but diverge on constitutional supremacy, remedial power and appointments.

**Why this earns marks:** It compares like dimensions and anchors the central distinction in constitutional structure.

## Q5. ‘Once a Speaker, Always a Speaker’! Do you think this practice should be adopted to impart objectivity to the office of the Speaker of Lok Sabha? What could be its implications for the robust functioning of parliamentary business in India?
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Speaker’s neutrality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q5 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Assess adoption; Explain implications  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Speaker  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `once-speaker-always-speaker-neutrality`

**Demand decoding:** Evaluate transplantation of the British convention and connect neutrality to the Speaker’s concrete procedural and adjudicatory powers.

**Compact framework:** Convention → case for adoption → limits → institutional package.

### Model answer

The British convention expects the Speaker to sever party ties and seek re-election as a non-partisan presiding officer. India should adopt its principle, though not mechanically.

Neutrality is vital because the Speaker controls debate, certifies Money Bills under Article **110**, exercises a casting vote under Article **100**, and decides Tenth Schedule petitions. Return to party politics creates an appearance of future partisan reward. *Kihoto Hollohan* made such decisions reviewable; *Keisham Meghachandra Singh* (2020) exposed harmful delay.

A convention of resignation from party posts, non-partisan re-election, secure tenure and a cooling-off period could improve trust, orderly debate and opposition participation.

Unlike Britain, India has intense multiparty competition and no settled uncontested-re-election convention. Neutrality also requires transparent rulings and time limits for defection cases.

**Conclusion:** “Always a Speaker” is desirable as an institutional ethic, but must be supported by enforceable procedural reforms.

**Why this earns marks:** It answers both limbs and ties the convention to the Speaker’s constitutionally important powers.

## Q6. In order to enhance the prospects of social development, sound and adequate health care policies are needed particularly in the fields of geriatric and maternal health care. Discuss.
**Primary route:** Social Justice → [Health Systems, Public Health and Universal Health Coverage](../../topics/social-justice/health-systems-public-health-and-universal-health-coverage.md) → Geriatric and maternal care · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q6 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Social Justice → Health → Life-course healthcare  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Elderly and women  
**Canonical owners:** [Elderly and Senior Citizens](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/12_Elderly-and-Senior-Citizens.md) · [Health Systems](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/03_Health-Systems-Public-Health-and-Universal-Health-Coverage.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `geriatric-maternal-health-social-development`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why both fields are social-development multipliers, identify policy gaps, and propose an integrated life-course response.

**Compact framework:** Social rationale → maternal policy → geriatric policy → common system reforms.

### Model answer

Maternal and geriatric care address life stages where vulnerability, unpaid care and impoverishment converge. They operationalise Articles **21, 42 and 47** and advance equality, longevity and human capital.

Maternal policy must ensure antenatal screening, nutrition, skilled birth attendance, emergency obstetric referral, safe abortion, postnatal care and reproductive choice. Janani Suraksha Yojana, Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram and Pradhan Mantri Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan improve access, but quality, anaemia, transport and regional inequality remain concerns.

Ageing requires prevention and management of non-communicable diseases, mental-health and dementia care, rehabilitation, palliative care, assistive devices and protection from neglect. The National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly provides a framework, but specialist staff and community reach are limited.

Both require stronger primary-care teams, trained caregivers, medicines, home-based care, financial protection and convergence with nutrition and social security.

**Conclusion:** Sound policy must move from episodic schemes to universal, gender-sensitive and age-friendly primary care.

**Why this earns marks:** It links health interventions to social development and supplies policy-specific evidence for both groups.

## Q7. “Institutional quality is a crucial driver of economic performance.” In this context, suggest reforms in Civil Service for strengthening democracy.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Civil Services](../../topics/governance/civil-services.md) → Administrative reform · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q7 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Suggest reforms  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Civil Services → Democratic institutional quality  
**Canonical owners:** [Civil Services and Mission Karmayogi](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/basic/09_Civil-Services-and-Mission-Karmayogi.md) · [Ministries and Departments](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Ministries-and-Departments-of-Government.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `civil-service-reform-democratic-institutions`

**Demand decoding:** Connect administrative quality with development and propose reforms that improve both performance and democratic accountability.

**Compact framework:** Institutional link → neutrality/capacity/accountability reforms → safeguards.

### Model answer

Capable and impartial administration lowers transaction costs, delivers public goods and creates trust; weak institutions convert policy into discretion and rent-seeking. Reform must strengthen capacity and accountability.

- **Professionalism:** competency-based training under Mission Karmayogi, specialised streams and regulated lateral entry.
- **Stability and neutrality:** minimum tenure and Civil Services Boards, consistent with *T.S.R. Subramanian v. Union of India* (2013), to reduce arbitrary transfers and oral instructions.
- **Performance:** outcome-linked appraisal and independent evaluation without reducing service to numerical targets.
- **Integrity:** transparent postings, conflict disclosure, effective vigilance and whistle-blower safeguards.
- **Citizen orientation:** service standards, grievance appeal, social audits and participatory design.

The Second Administrative Reforms Commission advocated ethics, citizen-centricity and results-oriented administration. Articles **309–311** protect lawful independence, but cannot become shields for non-performance.

**Conclusion:** A politically neutral yet democratically responsive civil service is the institutional bridge between constitutional promises and inclusive growth.

**Why this earns marks:** The reforms are organised around capacity, neutrality, integrity and citizen control rather than generic efficiency.

## Q8. “The emergence of Fourth Industrial Revolution (Digital Revolution) has initiated e-Governance as an integral part of government.” Discuss.
**Primary route:** Governance → [E-Governance Models and User-Centricity](../../topics/governance/e-governance-models-and-user-centricity.md) → Fourth Industrial Revolution · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q8 · 10 marks · 150 words · Difficulty: Moderate · Demand tag: Discuss  
**Primary classification:** Governance → E-Governance → Digital transformation  
**Canonical owner:** [E-Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/05_E-Governance-Models-and-User-Centricity.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `fourth-industrial-revolution-e-governance`

**Demand decoding:** Explain how 4IR technologies change core government functions while evaluating exclusion, privacy and accountability risks.

**Compact framework:** Technology shift → governmental applications → gains → risks and safeguards.

### Model answer

The Fourth Industrial Revolution integrates cloud computing, artificial intelligence, big data, mobile platforms and automation. It makes e-governance part of how government identifies beneficiaries, regulates, purchases and decides.

India’s shift from the National e-Governance Plan to Digital India illustrates this. Aadhaar-enabled direct benefit transfer can reduce duplication; GSTN integrates tax administration; GeM digitises procurement; telemedicine and online courts reduce distance; analytics support surveillance and disaster response.

The gains are speed, traceability, interoperability and lower transaction costs. Yet technology can also automate exclusion, intensify surveillance, reproduce biased data and marginalise citizens lacking devices, connectivity or literacy. Cyber insecurity and opaque algorithms weaken accountability.

Under *K.S. Puttaswamy*, privacy intrusions require legality, legitimate purpose, necessity and proportionality. Assisted access, offline alternatives, data minimisation, audits and appeals against automated decisions are essential.

**Conclusion:** 4IR makes digital government integral, but only human-centred design can make it democratic.

**Why this earns marks:** It demonstrates structural transformation, gives Indian applications and qualifies efficiency with rights safeguards.

**Demand decoding:** Assess operational contributions against mandate and structural constraints, then identify reforms rather than assigning monocausal blame.

**Compact framework:** Mandate/actions → shortcomings → structural explanation → reform.

### Model answer

WHO coordinates international health under its Constitution and the **International Health Regulations, 2005**. It declared a Public Health Emergency on **30 January 2020**, characterised COVID-19 as a pandemic on **11 March**, issued guidance, coordinated the Solidarity Trial and co-led ACT-Accelerator/COVAX.

Reform requires stronger outbreak-verification powers, reliable assessed financing, transparent emergency review, empowered country offices, resilient regional surveillance and enforceable duties for timely data-sharing.

**Conclusion:** WHO was indispensable but insufficient: its technical coordination mitigated the crisis, while state dependence exposed a weak global health-security architecture.

## Q11. Indian Constitution exhibits centralising tendencies to maintain unity and integrity of the nation. Elucidate in the perspective of the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897; The Disaster Management Act, 2005 and recently passed Farm Acts.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Centre-State & Inter-State Relations](../../topics/polity/centre-state-relations.md) → Centralising tendencies · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q11 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Elucidate through statutes  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Federalism → Legislative and administrative centralisation  
**Secondary classification:** Disaster Management → Legal architecture  
**Canonical owners:** [Centre–State Relations](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/13_Centre-State-and-Inter-State-Relations.md) · [Disaster Management Architecture](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Disaster-Management/basic/02_Indian-Legal-and-Institutional-Architecture.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `centralising-tendencies-epidemic-disaster-farm-laws`

**Demand decoding:** Establish the Constitution’s Union tilt, show how each 2020 statute/response manifested it, and evaluate centralisation against federal consultation.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional design → epidemic law → disaster law → farm laws → federal qualification.

### Model answer

India is federal in distribution of powers but contains centralising devices intended to preserve common security, markets and constitutional governance. Parliament controls the Union and Concurrent Lists under Article **246**, residuary power under Article **248**, and may legislate through Articles **249, 250, 252 and 253**. Articles **256–257** strengthen Union administrative coordination.

**Pandemic legislation**

- The **Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897** primarily empowers State governments, while Section 2A gives the Union specified powers concerning international travel and ports. Its sparse framework required extensive executive coordination.
- The **Disaster Management Act, 2005** enabled a nationally coordinated lockdown. Sections **6, 10 and 35** gave the National Disaster Management Authority, National Executive Committee and Union Government broad policy and coordination powers, while States implemented directions under Section 38. Uniform action helped contain inter-State spillovers, but sudden central directions exposed migrant, livelihood and consultation deficits.

**Farm Acts**

Parliament grounded the 2020 laws principally in Concurrent List Entry **33** concerning trade and commerce in specified products and Union competence over inter-State trade. States argued that agriculture, markets and land substantially fall under State List Entries **14, 28 and 18**, and that the laws displaced APMC-based regulatory choices without adequate consultation.

Thus centralisation may solve collective-action problems, ensure national standards and maintain a common market. Yet legal competence alone does not create cooperative legitimacy. The Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions emphasised consultation and restrained use of Union superiority.

**Conclusion:** India’s constitutional centre is strong by design, but emergency and market integration should operate through transparent necessity, subsidiarity, fiscal support and institutional consultation. Otherwise unity-producing centralisation can become federal alienation.

**Why this earns marks:** It connects exact constitutional and statutory provisions with the contemporary federal controversy and supplies a qualified verdict.

## Q12. Judicial Legislation is antithetical to the doctrine of separation of powers as envisaged in the Indian Constitution. In this context, justify the filing of large number of public interest petitions praying for issuing guidelines to executive authorities.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Supreme Court](../../topics/polity/supreme-court.md) → PIL and judicial guidelines · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q12 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tags: Examine premise; Justify PIL guidelines  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Judiciary → Judicial activism and separation of powers  
**Canonical owner:** [Supreme Court](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/18_Supreme-Court.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `pil-judicial-guidelines-separation-of-powers`

**Demand decoding:** Reconcile the apparent contradiction: courts cannot legislate, yet rights-enforcing interim guidelines may be justified when the political branches leave a vacuum.

**Compact framework:** Indian separation → justification → precedents → limits → test.

### Model answer

India follows functional separation, not watertight compartmentalisation. In *Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab* (1955), the Supreme Court recognised overlapping functions while preserving institutional boundaries. Courts interpret law and enforce rights under Articles **32 and 226**; ordinary policy and legislation belong to elected branches.

Public-interest petitions seeking guidelines are nevertheless justified where:

1. executive inaction exposes vulnerable groups to continuing fundamental-rights violations;
2. no effective claimant can approach the court individually;
3. an acknowledged legal vacuum prevents enforcement of an existing constitutional or statutory duty; and
4. temporary minimum standards are necessary until the competent authority acts.

In *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* (1997), guidelines against workplace sexual harassment filled a legislative vacuum by drawing upon Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 and CEDAW; they operated until Parliament enacted the 2013 law. *Vineet Narain* (1997) issued directions to insulate corruption investigations, while *Prakash Singh* (2006) prescribed police-reform safeguards after prolonged governmental inaction.

However, PIL cannot convert courts into continuing administrators. Guidelines must be rights-anchored, evidence-based, workable, temporary and open to legislative replacement. Article **142** supplements complete justice but cannot erase substantive law. Courts should seek institutional expertise, hear affected stakeholders and avoid choices involving resource allocation or competing policy models unless constitutional minima are breached.

**Conclusion:** Judicial legislation as a substitute for representative law-making is objectionable; judicially crafted interim safeguards are legitimate when narrowly necessary to prevent a rights vacuum. Their democratic legitimacy lies in triggering, not permanently displacing, executive and legislative action.

**Why this earns marks:** It resolves the question’s tension through doctrine, leading cases and a limiting test.

## Q13. The strength and sustenance of local institutions in India has shifted from their formative phase of ‘Functions, Functionaries and Funds’ to the contemporary stage of ‘Functionality’. Highlight the critical challenges faced by local institutions in terms of their functionality in recent times.
**Primary route:** Governance → [Local Governance and Service Delivery](../../topics/governance/local-governance-and-service-delivery.md) → Institutional functionality · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q13 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Highlight challenges  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Local Government → Functionality and service delivery  
**Secondary classification:** Polity → Panchayati Raj and Municipalities  
**Canonical owners:** [Local Governance](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/12_Local-Governance-and-Service-Delivery.md) · [Panchayati Raj](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/23_Panchayati-Raj.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `local-institutions-functionality-challenges`

**Demand decoding:** Move beyond the formal 3Fs to diagnose whether local bodies can convert authority and resources into accountable outcomes.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional foundation → autonomy/capacity/accountability/coordination challenges → reforms.

### Model answer

The 73rd and 74th Amendments created democratic local institutions, but Articles **243G and 243W** merely enable States to devolve responsibilities related to the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules. “Functionality” asks whether local bodies can actually plan, decide, implement and remain answerable for services.

**Critical challenges**

- **Incomplete activity mapping:** Functions remain fragmented across State departments, parastatals and special-purpose vehicles; responsibility is separated from control.
- **Weak personnel authority:** Local bodies often lack engineers, planners, accountants and public-health staff, while State cadres remain answerable upward.
- **Fiscal dependence:** Narrow own-revenue bases, poor property-tax administration, tied grants and delayed transfers impair initiative. State Finance Commissions under Articles **243I and 243Y** are often delayed or weakly implemented.
- **Planning deficits:** Gram plans, municipal plans and District/Metropolitan Planning Committees under Articles **243ZD–243ZE** are poorly integrated with departmental budgets.
- **Democratic deficits:** Irregular meetings, weak ward committees and gram sabhas, elite capture, proxy representation and limited disclosure reduce participation.
- **Capacity and scale:** Small rural bodies and rapidly expanding cities struggle with procurement, digital systems, climate risks, sanitation and complex infrastructure.
- **Accountability confusion:** Multiple audits and approvals coexist with weak outcome measurement; citizens cannot identify which agency is responsible.

Reform requires clear subsidiarity-based activity maps, local control over staff, buoyant own revenues, regular SFCs, untied predictable grants, professional municipal cadres, empowered ward/gram institutions and public outcome dashboards. Parallel agencies must report to elected local governments.

**Conclusion:** The next generation of decentralisation is not another list of functions; it is an enforceable operating system joining authority, capability, finance and citizen accountability.

**Why this earns marks:** It directly interprets “functionality” and organises recent challenges by institutional mechanism.

## Q14. Rajya Sabha has been transformed from a ‘useless stepney tyre’ to the most useful supporting organ in past few decades. Highlight the factors as well as the areas in which this transformation could be visible.
**Primary route:** Polity → [Parliament](../../topics/polity/parliament.md) → Rajya Sabha · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q14 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Highlight factors and areas  
**Primary classification:** Polity → Parliament → Council of States  
**Canonical owner:** [Parliament](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/advanced/17_Parliament.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `rajya-sabha-transformation`

**Demand decoding:** Explain why the chamber’s political relevance increased and where its constitutional, legislative and deliberative value is visible.

**Compact framework:** Constitutional design → transformation factors → visible areas → limitations.

### Model answer

Rajya Sabha was criticised as a delaying, indirectly elected chamber. Yet Articles **80 and 83(1)** make it a permanent Council of States, not a duplicate Lok Sabha.

**Factors behind transformation**

- Decline of one-party dominance and rise of coalition and regional parties made Upper House consent politically consequential.
- Different electoral cycles frequently produced a composition unlike the Lok Sabha, checking transient majorities.
- Experienced and nominated members enriched specialised deliberation.
- Complex regulation increased the value of committees and revision.

**Areas where utility is visible**

1. **Federal voice:** State-based representation brings regional interests into national legislation.
2. **Legislative revision:** The House can amend, delay or refer ordinary Bills to Select Committees, exposing drafting and implementation concerns.
3. **Executive scrutiny:** Questions, short-duration discussions, motions and committees sustain accountability even when the government commands the Lok Sabha.
4. **Continuity:** As a permanent chamber, it preserves institutional memory during Lok Sabha dissolution.
5. **Special powers:** Articles **249** and **312** permit it to enable Union legislation on State subjects and new All India Services. It has equal power in constitutional amendments.

Its limits remain: no-confidence belongs to Lok Sabha, Money Bills largely bypass it, and obstruction can become partisan.

**Conclusion:** Rajya Sabha is most useful not as a rival government-making chamber but as a federal, revising and continuity institution. Respect for bicameral scrutiny—and restraint in Money Bill classification—determines whether this transformation endures.

**Why this earns marks:** It separately answers the causes and manifestations of transformation with exact constitutional powers.

**Primary route:** Polity → [National Commissions for SCs, STs and Backward Classes](../../topics/polity/national-commissions-sc-st-bc.md) → National Commission for Women · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Women and gender justice  
**Canonical owners:** [National Commissions](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/National-Commissions-SC-ST-BC.md) · [Women and Gender Justice](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Social-Justice/basic/05_Women-and-Gender-Justice.md) · [Statutory Bodies](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Polity/basic/Statutory-Regulatory-and-Quasi-Judicial-Bodies.md)

**Demand decoding:** State the Article 368 route and design choices, then assess whether constitutional status is sufficient—not merely desirable.

**Compact framework:** Amendment procedure → constitutional design → gains → limits → conditions.

### Model answer

The National Commission for Women is a statutory body under the **National Commission for Women Act, 1990**. Constitutionalisation requires an Article **368** amendment passed in each House by a total-membership majority and two-thirds present and voting. State ratification applies only if the proviso to Article 368 is attracted.

The amendment should not merely insert the Commission’s name. It must specify:

- composition, expertise and inclusive representation;
- a transparent, bipartisan appointment process;
- fixed tenure, removal safeguards and non-diminution of service conditions;

## Q16. “The incidence and intensity of poverty are more important in determining poverty than income alone.” In this context analyse the latest United Nations Multidimensional Poverty Index Report.
**Primary route:** Economy → [Growth, Development, HDI, IHDI and MPI](../../topics/economy/growth-development-hdi-ihdi-and-mpi.md) → Multidimensional Poverty Index · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q16 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: High · Demand tag: Analyse report through concept  
**Primary classification:** Economy → Development → Multidimensional poverty  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Poverty measurement  
**Canonical owner:** [Growth, Development and MPI](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Economy/basic/02_Growth-Development-HDI-IHDI-and-MPI.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `un-multidimensional-poverty-index-2020`

**Demand decoding:** Explain incidence and intensity, apply the 2020 UNDP–OPHI findings, and compare MPI’s policy value with income poverty without claiming substitution.

**Compact framework:** Measurement logic → report findings → analytical value → limitations.

### Model answer

Income poverty asks whether resources fall below a monetary line; multidimensional poverty asks which basic capabilities are simultaneously denied. The global MPI, produced by **UNDP and the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative**, uses ten indicators across health, education and living standards.

Its Alkire–Foster structure captures:

- **incidence (H):** the proportion of people who are multidimensionally poor; and
- **intensity (A):** the average share of weighted deprivations suffered by each poor person.

Thus **MPI = H × A**. Two regions with the same poverty headcount may require different policy responses if one experiences deeper overlapping deprivation.

The **Global MPI 2020**, drawing on the latest available surveys, covered 107 developing countries and found about 1.3 billion people multidimensionally poor. It highlighted that children bore a disproportionate burden and that deprivation commonly clustered in nutrition, schooling, sanitation, cooking fuel, housing and electricity. For India, it reported that about **273 million people** moved out of multidimensional poverty between 2005/06 and 2015/16—major progress, while substantial subnational and child deprivation remained.

MPI improves policy by revealing *who* is poor, *how* they are poor and whether progress reduces both prevalence and depth. It supports district-level convergence: a household deprived in nutrition, sanitation and schooling needs coordinated services, not income support alone.

Yet MPI depends on survey periodicity, chosen weights and available indicators; it may miss intra-household inequality, vulnerability and sudden income shocks. The 2020 report’s pre-pandemic data could not capture COVID-19 reversals.

**Conclusion:** Incidence and intensity make poverty analysis capability-sensitive, but MPI should complement income, consumption and vulnerability measures rather than replace them.

**Why this earns marks:** It defines the index mathematically, uses report-specific findings and gives both policy value and methodological qualification.

## Q17. “Micro-Finance as an anti-poverty vaccine, is aimed at asset creation and income security of the rural poor in India.” Evaluate the role of the Self Help Groups in achieving the twin objectives along with empowering women in rural India.
**Primary route:** Governance → [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../topics/governance/ngos-shgs-and-civil-society-stakeholders.md) → Women’s SHGs and microfinance · **Backlink:** [Paper-wise index](../../catalogue/PAPERWISE-INDEX.md)

**Metadata:** 2020 · Mains · GS-II · Official Q17 · 15 marks · 250 words · Difficulty: Moderate-high · Demand tag: Evaluate  
**Primary classification:** Governance → Development Processes → Self Help Groups  
**Secondary classification:** Social Justice → Women’s empowerment and poverty  
**Canonical owner:** [NGOs, SHGs and Civil Society Stakeholders](../../../upsc-ai-kit/knowledge/Governance/advanced/04_NGOs-SHGs-and-Civil-Society-Stakeholders.md)  
**Proposed topic slug:** `shg-microfinance-assets-income-women`

**Demand decoding:** Test all three claimed outcomes—asset creation, income security and empowerment—through mechanisms, evidence and limitations.

**Compact framework:** SHG mechanism → economic outcomes → empowerment → constraints → enabling package.

### Model answer

Self Help Groups pool savings, use peer-based lending and connect poor women to formal finance. NABARD’s **SHG–Bank Linkage Programme** (1992) and **DAY-NRLM** have scaled this architecture.

**Asset creation:** Group credit can finance livestock, equipment, shops and processing. Joint procurement and producer collectives lower costs and improve market access. Kerala’s Kudumbashree demonstrates group-based micro-enterprise.

**Income security:** Savings and revolving funds smooth consumption and reduce dependence on moneylenders. Diversified livelihoods, emergency credit and collective marketing make households more resilient to seasonal shocks. Group records can also facilitate insurance, pensions and bank access.

**Women’s empowerment:** Meetings expand mobility, financial literacy and public voice. Control over savings improves household bargaining; federations enable participation in gram sabhas and local governance.

Enterprises face weak skills, technology, infrastructure and markets. Unpaid group work may burden women, while male relatives may control loans. The poorest or socially excluded women may remain outside viable groups.

Therefore credit must be combined with livelihood planning, skilling, producer organisations, market and digital access, interest transparency, borrower-protection rules, insurance and social services. Bank linkage should reward enterprise quality, not merely loan volume.

**Conclusion:** SHGs are effective poverty and empowerment institutions when finance is embedded in collective capability and market support; credit alone only relocates risk to poor women.

**Why this earns marks:** It evaluates each objective separately and qualifies the causal claim with a concrete enabling framework.

**Demand decoding:** Correct both labels in the proposition, explain the widening agenda visible by 2020, and assess its strategic-economic trajectory.

**Compact framework:** Origins → why not alliance → why not trade bloc → widening agenda → India’s interest.

### Model answer

The Quad—India, Australia, Japan and the United States—grew from cooperation after the 2004 tsunami, convened in 2007 and revived in 2017. It supports a free, open and rules-based Indo-Pacific, but neither label in the proposition is accurate.

**Not a military alliance:** The Quad has no collective-defence treaty, integrated command, permanent secretariat or automatic war obligation. The four countries participate in naval exercises—Australia joined the Malabar exercise in 2020—and share maritime concerns, but India retains strategic autonomy. **Why this earns marks:** It challenges the false binary, defines both institutional forms and explains the actual trajectory.
